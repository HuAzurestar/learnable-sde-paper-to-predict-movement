\title{Horizon-dependent Benefits and Reliability Limits\\of Mixture-SDE Movement Forecasting}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
A movement forecast can improve its distributional score without locating
the target more accurately or providing reliable uncertainty regions. We
examine these distinctions in an existing evaluation of fitted stochastic
movement predictors applied to historical GPX position recordings with
unverified carrier identities and transport modes, using 46 retrospectively selected recording-hash
blocks, five simulation seeds and 512 particles per ensemble. At nominal
1 and 5 minutes, deterministic constant-speed extrapolation has lower
energy score (ES) than the heading-stratified mixture SDE (HSM-SDE,
registered as Full01); at 15 and 30 minutes the ordering reverses.
Averaging the four targets gives ES \FullES\ m for HSM-SDE and
\InertialES\ m for constant-velocity extrapolation (CV). A residual-direction
mixture variant (RDM-SDE, registered as GMM) has lower average ES and final
mean-position error, but its paired ES interval includes zero. Observation
intervals of 120 and 300 saved seconds yield larger average ES changes than
the examined training/adaptation variants relative to their respective
controls; this is not a direct test between those design classes. The 300 s
variant reduces average ES by 23.40 m but increases final mean-position
error by 9.64 m. HSM-SDE's nominal 90\% prediction disk
covers only 46.52\% of 30-minute targets. These are conditional descriptive
findings: participant independence and physical clock provenance remain
unverified, and the cohort was selected using future-route map support.
Required forecast failures prevent terrain-family inference. The study
identifies conditional long-horizon utility and design trade-offs, not the
necessity of SDE complexity or a confirmed structural winner. It demonstrates
an executable probabilistic forecasting procedure, but does not establish
reliable long-horizon regions or superiority over a simple probabilistic baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}\hypertarget{sec:introduction}{}
Movement prediction is often summarized by a single accuracy measure, yet
three distinct tasks are involved: assigning a distribution to future
positions, locating a representative position, and describing a region
that contains the realized target at its stated frequency. A gain on one
task need not imply a gain on the others. This distinction matters when a
probabilistic forecast is interpreted as a reliable search region.

Continuous-time correlated random walks model movement persistence and
irregular observations~\cite{johnson2008}; switching state-space models
represent latent regimes~\cite{ghahramani2000}. Step-selection models link
movement to environmental selection~\cite{avgar2016,avgar2017correction}.
Learned trajectory predictors~\cite{gupta2018,salzmann2020}, neural SDE
generators~\cite{kidger2021} and interpretable stochastic system
identification~\cite{gao2024} offer additional modelling approaches.
These works provide context, not performance baselines executed here.
Our empirical subject is future position in selected geographic recordings;
the modelling object is a conditional distribution over those positions.
A stochastic position model represents uncertainty through a fitted drift,
residual diffusion and latent components. The study evaluates an existing,
limited collection of these fitted predictors.

Proper scoring rules evaluate predictive distributions against realized
targets~\cite{gneiting2007,gneiting2008}; they do not certify coverage of
a particular prediction region. We therefore ask how distributional
score, error of the predictive mean and marginal disk coverage change
across four horizons. Our primary concern is the utility and reliability
of longer-horizon forecasts, with shorter horizons providing a contrast.
The argument proceeds through four questions: when does the stochastic
reference improve on constant-velocity extrapolation; what do alternative
component constructions show; how do observation-interval and
training/adaptation choices compare in their observed effects; and can
the resulting long-horizon prediction disks be trusted? Score--location
disagreement is used to interpret these choices, not treated as a separate
list of unrelated experimental outcomes.

This focus is an interpretation of existing results, not a newly
registered hypothesis test. The original questions remain explicit:
RQ1 asks whether the registered changes in mode structure, observation
interval, fitting/adaptation and numerical prediction yield a practically
useful distribution-score change relative to their named controls. RQ2 asks
whether selected terrain groups add predictive value beyond a fitted
history baseline, through overall, leave-one-group-out and leave-one-group-in
comparisons. Those registered questions and inference rules are retained.
The four questions organising the present paper are descriptive readings
of the existing evidence, not additional registered tests. We first define
the recordings and forecast task, explain the model construction and
evaluation criteria, then move from horizon-specific utility through design
choices to region reliability. Terrain efficacy is reported separately as
an unresolved question, not a demonstrated benefit of the ordinary SDE.

\subsection{Evaluation population and clock}
The task is single-recording position forecasting from a visible trajectory
prefix. The retained data pipeline identifies its source as a historical
OpenStreetMap GPX harvest, not the separate GeoLife cohort used in earlier
work. User identities, transport-mode labels and original GPX segment
boundaries are not preserved; these records cannot be treated as verified
pedestrian participants. R1-derived segments supply midpoint history/target
windows. The frozen recording-hash assignment separates development and
evaluation: qualified development contains 404 outer-training windows
(328 training and 76 adaptation) and 81 validation windows. Terrain fitting
uses one first-future transition per outer-training window. Exact identity
intersections across splits are zero, which does not establish participant
or near-route independence. The \supp{sec:protocol} details this assignment.

The released final-evaluation split contains 12,370 windows from 1,094 recording-hash
blocks. Temporal eligibility retains 127 windows/89 blocks, joint feature
validity retains 106/73, and the fixed primary-origin rule selects 46/46
(Figure~\ref{fig:population}). A block identifies a saved recording, not
a verified independent participant. The selected cohort spans 12 countries
and 27 source labels; 33/46 blocks are from Great Britain, Belgium or
Luxembourg. It is not a representative population sample.

Future observations are used to select this cohort and to score forecasts,

This distinction prevents direct target leakage during rollout without
removing the retrospective selection restriction. The evaluation data had
previously been inspected; the full influence of that inspection on design
choices is not recoverable. This is not a fresh blinded holdout.

Targets are the actual observations matched to nominal 1, 5, 15 and 30
minutes, with elapsed-time ranges 49--67, 294--305, 889--910 and
1785--1814 saved seconds. No target is interpolated to an exact nominal
time. Physical acquisition-time and UTC provenance are unverified. An
absolute offset affects the calendar input; a relative-scale distortion
also affects rates, diffusion units and the physical interpretation of
the horizons. All minute labels below refer to the saved clock.

\subsection{Mechanism-based model names and construction}\label{sec:models}\hypertarget{sec:models}{}
We call the ordinary three-mode reference the heading-stratified mixture
SDE (HSM-SDE), because its fitting batches are divided by segment heading.
Its registered identifier is Full01; ``Full'' in retained equations, plots
and tables denotes this reference, not the all-terrain configuration.
The residual-direction mixture SDE (RDM-SDE) is the registered GMM variant;
its groups are constructed once from residual directions, not by fitting
a conventional GMM with expectation--maximisation. Both are mixture-SDE
procedures, so their comparison is within that family, not GMM versus SDE.
The deterministic reference is constant-velocity extrapolation (CV), saved
as Inertial/I. These explanatory names do not change any model, saved
identity or comparison control. In saved local east/north coordinates $X=(X_e,X_n)^\top$
(metres), it uses
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ordinary-full}
 dX_t=\Theta_{m_t}^{\top}\psi(X_t,t)\,dt+L_{m_t}\,dW_t,
 \quad \psi=(1,X_e,X_n,S)^\top,\quad L_mL_m^\top=Q_m=\tau R_m.
\end{equation}
\input{reader-v4-full.tex}
The first visible position anchors the coordinate frame; forecasting starts
at the last permitted prefix position. $S$ is a solar/calendar proxy computed
from the retained absolute tick~\cite{noaa}, not a validated daylight
mechanism. $R_m$ is a centred fitted displacement-rate residual covariance;
its conversion uses observation interval $\tau$, not integration step $h$.
Each particle draws a mode from saved weights $\pi$ at the origin and
segment-fixed Full retains that mode. Given frame, position, clock and
random stream, ordinary Full does not read initial speed or recent heading.
For the 46 primary origins, the origin-state buffer retains the last three
observed positions and their strictly increasing saved times. The origin
is the last point. CV extrapolates
$\widehat x^{\rm I}(u)=x_0+u\widehat v_0$, where $\widehat v_0$ is the secant
from the first to last of those three points, divided by their saved elapsed
time; no future position or future speed is used. These spans range from
2 to 61 saved seconds (median 7). HSM-SDE and CV thus receive
the same permitted prefix but use its movement information differently.

The validation selection proxy is a one-step
rate-residual Gaussian using the unweighted average mode covariance, whereas
evaluation scores a multi-mode, multi-step position distribution. The GMM
variant groups training residuals once by $\arg\max(r_e,r_n,-r_e-r_n)$ but
uses segment-heading labels for validation components: these labels are not
aligned. Independently heading-sorted batches are also mixed by mode index
in the transfer procedures~\cite{nichol2018}. These are implementation
limitations, not evidence that the corresponding modelling ideas fail.

Evaluation uses $N=512$ particles, five fixed seeds and maximum integration
step $h=5$ saved seconds. Full uses conditional Gaussian moment propagation,
evaluating nonlinear conditions at the conditional mean; this is not an
exact solution of a globally nonlinear SDE~\cite{kloeden1992}. Four targets
come from a continuing joint path, not independently sampled marginals.
Observation-interval variants change fitting data, $Q=\tau R$ and update
cadence together while retaining the integration cap. In particular, dt300
and dt600 mean observation intervals of 300 and 600 saved seconds, not
integration steps. Identical-parameter aliases and multi-factor changes
make the 28 saved identities procedures rather than 28 independent algorithms.
All 21 registered primary contrasts retain their named Full controls.

\section{Evaluation measures and inference}
For particles $x_1,\ldots,x_N$ and target $y$ at one matched horizon, the
execution-bound finite-ensemble energy score is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-es}
 \ES_N=\frac1N\sum_i\|x_i-y\|
       -\frac1{2N(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}\|x_i-x_j\|,\qquad N\geq2.
\end{equation}
The second sum uses ordered pairs. For the deterministic inertial reference,
ES is directly $\|x-y\|$. ES is measured in metres and lower is better;
weighted ES is the equally weighted average over the four targets.
Mean-position error is $e_j=\|\bar x_j-y_j\|$, with
$\mathrm{ADE}=\tfrac14\sum_j e_j$ and $\mathrm{FDE}=e_4$.
FDE is neither best-particle nor average-particle error, and ADE is not a
dense-path integral. ES includes interparticle spread and cannot be
converted into error of the predictive mean.
The first ES term measures particle distances to the target; the second
subtracts half the average interparticle distance. Their balance evaluates
the distribution, rather than the distance from its centre alone.
Consequently, changing spread or mixture geometry can change ES without
moving the mean closer to the target. This motivates reporting both
quantities rather than using ES as a proxy for localisation.

A nominal level-$q$ disk is centred at the ensemble mean, with radius the
$\lceil qN\rceil$-th ordered distance of particles from that mean. Coverage
records whether the true target lies inside; area is $\pi r^2$.
Mean area is $\mathrm E[\pi r^2]$, not $\pi\mathrm E[r]^2$. These are
horizon-wise marginal regions, not joint path regions. Enclosing about
90\% of simulated particles does not guarantee 90\% coverage of real targets.
For multimodal clouds, the mean may lie in a low-density gap and a disk may
include inaccessible terrain; it is not a highest-density or route-constrained
region.
For $\widehat P_j$'s own disk $D_{q,j}$, nominal mass concerns
$\widehat P_j(D_{q,j})\approx q$. Empirical reliability instead concerns
the observed frequency of $Y_j\in D_{q,j}$ across origins. The two coincide
only under suitable calibration of the predictive distribution; the
particle construction itself supplies no such guarantee. Area records
how much spatial information that coverage costs.

For each recording block, the five seed values are averaged first; blocks
then receive equal weight. There are 46 targets per horizon, not 230
independent targets. Six known-velocity and six point-only cases reuse
primary records, giving 58 origin--mode cases rather than 58 independent
blocks. Original family-wise simultaneous bootstrap-$t$ intervals use
2,000 draws per stream, with shared block indices within a family;
Holm-adjusted zero tests have a separate role~\cite{holm1979,davison1997}.
Intervals condition on the saved fits and unverified block independence /
exchangeability; seeds represent simulation variation, not training or
participant replication.

Deltas are candidate minus named control. The registered practical margin
is $\delta=41.100259$ m, 5\% of validation inertial ES, not a validated
operational utility threshold. Practical benefit/harm requires the entire
original interval beyond $-\delta$/$+\delta$ and the original statistical,
seed and qualification gates. Crossing zero is not equivalence. Practical
equivalence, inconclusive information and unavailable evidence are distinct.
The \supp{sec:bootstrap-table-detail} retains precise endpoints and finite
bootstrap-tail limitations. The descriptive horizon and coverage summaries
below are not new superiority or calibration hypothesis tests.

\subsection{Design choices within the mixture-SDE family}\label{sec:design-choices}
\subsubsection{Component construction: a promising variant, not a confirmed winner}
RDM-SDE's average ES is 2.83 m lower than its named HSM-SDE control.
Its final mean-position error is also lower: 1299.11 versus 1326.09 m
(Table~\ref{tab:point-errors}). Its mean-position errors are lower at all
four saved targets, but that does not establish lower ES at each horizon.
All five seed ES differences are negative; the simultaneous block interval,
$[-21.22,16.33]$ m, nevertheless includes zero. Thus residual-direction
grouping is a potentially useful structural choice, not a confirmed
improvement or a universally best model.
The pointwise variant instead increases average ES by 32.95 m with interval
$[22.99,43.37]$ m. The single-Gaussian and explicit-feature variants change
ES by $+22.01$ and $+57.96$ m, with intervals including zero. None establishes
a registered practical benefit or harm. These comparisons concern fitted
procedures, not the general merit of mixtures or velocity information.

\subsubsection{Training initialisation and adaptation: no clear added score benefit}
Training initialisation and parameter adaptation are separate choices.
The scratch and Reptile variants change average ES by $+2.60$ and $+2.19$ m;
their intervals are $[-4.73,10.02]$ and $[-11.95,12.91]$ m. The drift-only and
two-step adaptation change ES by $+0.50$ and $-0.04$ m, with intervals
$[-1.52,2.49]$ and $[-2.60,1.81]$ m. Each is compared with its named Full11
control, not substituted into the Full01 horizon comparison. All four
original finite-procedure states are practically equivalent within the
registered margin. The examined training/adaptation complexity therefore
shows no clear added ES benefit; this is not evidence that learning itself
is unnecessary. Fixed fitted models and simulation seeds do not measure
variation across independently repeated training runs.

\subsection{Long-horizon prediction disks are not empirically reliable}\label{sec:region-reliability}
Generating a probability distribution and giving a trustworthy region are
different achievements. A nominal 90\% disk is intended to contain real
targets at roughly that frequency, not merely 90\% of simulated particles.
HSM-SDE's nominal 90\% disk coverage is 95.65\%, 37.83\%, 35.22\% and 46.52\%
at the four horizons. At one minute the nominal 50\% disk covers 33.04\%,
while at thirty minutes even the nominal 95\% disk covers only 52.17\%.
The departure therefore depends on horizon and probability level; no
radius rescaling has been tested. Across all 28 identities, thirty-minute
90\% coverage ranges from 21.74\% to 79.13\%. Thus, in this evaluation,
the thirty-minute HSM-SDE disk contains fewer than half of the targets,
despite its nominal 90\% level. Retained figure/table labels Full and GMM
refer to HSM-SDE and RDM-SDE respectively.

The 600 s procedure also has a larger thirty-minute mean area

\subsection{Inference qualification and unresolved terrain efficacy}\label{sec:terrain-results}\hypertarget{sec:terrain-results}{}
Of the 21 registered primary method contrasts, 14 have practically
equivalent and seven inconclusive finite-algorithm states; no original
simultaneous interval lies wholly beyond the practical-benefit or harm

states qualify the implemented finite procedures, not general algorithms.
For example, all five GMM seed effects are negative but its block interval

Three required primary terrain forecasts failed: all-terrain once and

\section{Discussion}\hypertarget{sec:discussion-score}{}
\subsection{What the model description lets us interpret}
The task definition connects the horizon result to the two reference
constructions. CV extends a short observed secant as a single straight
trajectory; it can remain accurate when that local rate persists, but any
rate error is multiplied by elapsed time. HSM-SDE instead assigns probability
to several fitted drift/diffusion components, without updating their weights
from the new prefix's heading. This gives different uses of recent movement
and different uncertainty representations. The observed short/long ordering
is consistent with a changing forecasting trade-off, but does not identify
which mechanism causes it: the comparison changes both mean dynamics and
distributional spread, and no task-matched probabilistic inertia control
isolates these effects. The constant-drift covariance explanation above is
not a fitted explanation of the observed reversal.

The other two diagnostics follow from the forecast summaries. ES evaluates
both distances to the realized target and distances within the ensemble,
whereas FDE evaluates only the final mean. An ES decrease with FDE increase
therefore establishes a disagreement between criteria, not a mathematical
contradiction. A radial particle quantile describes the model's own spread;
coverage compares that region with actual target positions. The observed
undercoverage rejects the interpretation of HSM-SDE's nominal disks as
empirically reliable regions for this cohort, even when average ES is
favourable. It does not identify whether drift error, insufficient spread,
mixture geometry or selection is responsible.

\subsection{Which design choices merit attention}
The observed long-horizon benefit makes the mixture-SDE family worth
examining beyond an average all-horizon score. Within it, RDM-SDE provides
a favourable descriptive structural result, whereas the training/adaptation
variants provide no clear added score benefit. Observation-interval choices
show larger saved mean ES changes, but can worsen mean-position error.
These observations prioritise design questions, not a global algorithm
ranking: the contrasts use their own controls, some change several factors,
and the original practical/inference qualifications still apply.

The objective-choice comparisons also require care. Full and QMLE retain
identical saved drift, covariance and mixture weights; mixed-loss and
pure-ES variants likewise retain identical saved parameters to each other.
Differences between those paired identities' finite scores do not prove
distinct learned predictive models or the superiority of one training loss.

\subsection{Reliability limits and remaining work}
The pipeline can fit and generate probabilistic forecasts and score them
at matched targets. The horizon result supplies conditional utility
evidence relative to a deterministic reference. Neither establishes a
reliable long-horizon prediction region: that requires empirical coverage
at the stated level while retaining informative area and location.
The coverage findings limit this capability even where average ES improves.

The calibration proxy differs from the evaluated predictive distribution,
and GMM training/validation component labels differ. Both are plausible
contributors to poor uncertainty behaviour, but these results do not
identify causes or validate a correction. Likewise, wider regions can
increase inclusion while retaining an inaccurate centre; coverage alone
would reward sufficiently uninformative regions. The metric conflict is
therefore substantive, not a reason to discard an unfavourable metric.

The scope is deliberately conditional. Retrospective future-route selection,
previous evaluation exposure, uncertain recording independence and unverified
clock provenance limit population and physical-time interpretation. Fixed
fits and seeds omit training uncertainty. Parameter aliases, unmatched
mode labels, joint procedure changes and representation-dependent penalties
limit component attribution. The missing matched probabilistic baseline
precludes a claim that complex stochastic structure is necessary. None of
these limitations is removed by reproducing saved numerical summaries.

Future work should compare a task-matched probabilistic motion baseline,
align mode labels before transfer and calibration, and evaluate calibration
of the actual multi-step distribution. Terrain comparisons require complete
families and matched effective penalties. Independent route grouping and
clock-source verification are needed for broader inference. These are
unexecuted studies, not results or editorial repairs supplied here.

\section{Conclusion}
This study yields four bounded conclusions about mixture-SDE movement
forecasting in 46 selected GPX recording blocks. First, HSM-SDE has lower
ES than CV at 15 and 30 saved minutes, while CV has lower ES at 1 and 5
minutes; its utility is horizon-dependent rather than universal. Second,
RDM-SDE has lower average ES and final mean-position error than HSM-SDE,
making residual-direction grouping a promising design choice, but its ES
interval includes zero and no structural winner is established. Third,
the observed 120/300 s interval changes exceed the examined training/
adaptation changes in mean ES magnitude against their respective controls;
extra training/adaptation complexity supplies no clear added ES benefit,
and the 300 s score gain comes with worse final mean location. Fourth,
the procedure generates probabilistic forecasts, but reliable long-horizon
disks are not established: HSM-SDE's nominal 90\% disk covers only 46.52\%
of thirty-minute targets. Lower average ES is therefore insufficient
evidence of a trustworthy prediction region.

For the original questions, RQ1 yields 14 practically equivalent and seven
inconclusive finite-procedure contrasts, with all resolution-invariant
judgements inconclusive. RQ2 remains unavailable because required terrain
forecasts failed under the registered complete-family rule. The learned
terrain mechanism is specified, but its specification is not evidence of
terrain efficacy. These results justify reporting horizon-specific ES,
mean-position error, coverage and area together; they do not establish
general algorithm superiority, the necessity of stochastic complexity or
an operational search guarantee. Unverified carrier identity and clock
provenance, retrospective selection and prior evaluation exposure bound
the population and physical-time interpretation of this conclusion.
